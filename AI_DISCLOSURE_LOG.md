# AI Disclosure

**TL;DR:** AI helped write the C99 and build telemetry harnesses; a human with actual DSP experience did all the reverse engineering, architecture, and hardware verification. There were zero autonomous agents and zero "prompt until it compiles" workflows here. Every change was strictly scoped, sandboxed, checked against original Motorola DSP56300 manuals, and verified in real retail games. Judge the fork by how your games sound.
---
Yes this project absolutely made use of AI assistance. However, unlike so many other recent Xemu forks and the plethora of new emulators and recompilations out there, this project did not use any hands-off autonomous AI agents whatsoever. The architecture and reverse engineering is 100% my own human effort and based on my professional subject matter expertise in this very specific domain. At no point did I ever "vibe" anything out, this was a brutal amount of (very fun) work that I am extremely proud of accomplishing and will continue supporting indefinitely. To be clear: I only put my efforts into the APU because that's all I know how to do. You will get absolutely no support or assistance from me on *any other Xbox subsystem* because I am quite frankly not qualified in those domains. I can do GPGPUs but I cannot do rasterization and the few documented fixes I did make outside of the audio subsystem were explicitly cherry-picked, vetted, tested, and curated by me for audio-adjacent reasons.

Now, what on earth did I use the AI for, you might be asking? Well I'm not a C99 programmer, which is what QEMU requires, and I am not making something from scratch -- I am carving out and integrating my ideas into someone else's code. And that "someone else" stretches decades past Xemu to XQEMU all the way to QEMU itself. I do have professional DSP experience so it wasn't hard to write out the pseudocode of what I needed to get done based on the original Motorola `DSP56300 FAMILY MANUAL 24-Bit Digital Signal Processor DSP56300FM/AD Revision 3.0` from November 2000 and the incredibly important, hyper-integral to this project `DSP56367UM Rev. 2.1` from 08/2006. This last "User's Manual" contains all the internal peripheral control registers such as the Host Interface (HDI08) specific register offsets, the memory map switching and core bit definitions, and the interrupt vector table offsets.

I mention this because the original DSP56362 User's Manual is (I'm officially declaring) **lost media**. Over the decades Motorola's semiconductor IP became the property of Freescale which became the property of NXP. The PDFs were exported with that era's version of FrameMaker, which makes it impossible to programmatically parse its table of contents for relevant information without converting it first through PyMuPDF with the Gemini API. Since this is all circa the early internet (1999-2001) that means *a lot* of material is probably gone for good unless a paper backup can be located in some long-forgotten corporate back office basement. Thankfully Motorola developed the Symphony audio DSP line as modular silicon blocks. They all share an identical 24-bit DSP56300 core and identical peripheral macrocells. The '367 UM is effectively the '362 UM with extra peripherals (like `DAX` and specific extra channels) appended, all we need to do is focus on the parts that the Xbox's DSP56362 shares.

So back to AI, I would not have been able to find this manual without leveraging Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG, aka AI Search). To give you an idea of what would have happened to this project had I not had this manual I only have to steer you [to this actual AI slop from upstream Xemu.](https://github.com/xemu-project/dsp56300/blob/main/docs/ARCHITECTURE-NOTES.md) When I first read it a couple months ago I assumed I must have been too stupid to understand it. Now re-reading it again after I've achieved feature completion, well, let's just say now I understand why Xemu is in the state that it is in.

So other than transpilation (I know MATLAB, Fortran, Python, and R so my pseudocode is a bit more deterministic than just prompting "convert my logic into code, make no mistakes") the other benefits of AI assistance were auditing Xemu's codebase to make for me a signal and logic map of how they implemented the APU (and rerunning those audits when they make changes) and allowing me to quickly query, search through, and reference my growing corpus of original research, notes, memory and bit mask maps, tracking progress and setbacks through every commit, and most importantly of all (to me) was just "red teaming" my logic every step of the way.

Not because the LLM chatbots had anything useful to say about my architecture or my approach. Actually the entire time they were begging me to "wait for upstream to implement it" and trying to gaslight me that they have the solution even though it's something I already investigated and ruled out. Most of the time their hallucinatory siren call helped me realize I'm wasting time going in a circle when I should be completely changing my approach and thinking outside the box. The truth is, LLMs can be surprisingly useless if you are doing something that doesn't either already exist in their training data or is readily accessible online. You must already possess the required skills and ability to steer the underlying mental model. And since I'm reverse engineering a black box and producing original research that no one else has and proving the "prior art" wrong, this would not be possible *with AI* had I not already known my stuff. LLMs don't set or enforce standards, only you can, because LLMs will absolutely forget those standards and drift into entropic madness without someone to police them constantly. LLMs can't define the scope of a project, only you can, otherwise they will assume any and all edge cases that the token budget allows them to and over-engineer a pile of slop. All of this requires some prior experience or understanding of software development and project management, because you can really get the ball rolling on what it is that you need doing if you know how to appropriately and specifically delegate tasks with the limitations of an LLM model front of mind. The core difference between delegating to a human developer and an AI is that for the former you are telling them what to *do* because you should trust their intuition and professional judgment, but for the latter you are telling them what *not to do* because you don't trust them at all.

All that being said, nothing currently beats an LLM when it comes to hunting down bugs and combing through code. My own eyes have missed so many unforced errors and mistakes because I felt like I could just go in and manually tweak things in the codebase myself rather than just using the trilateral multi-modal coding pipeline I instituted. To guard against causing syntax errors with a language I'm not as proficient in I basically handed what amounted to a Jira ticket to Gemini 3.8 Flash on the web to then convert that into a precise and bounded C code tasking prompt using a template I created to then copy that over to Antigravity for implementation. This prompt template contains a `SYSTEM RULES` header that lists the branch I'm working on, the `BASELINE STATE` achieved since the prior tasking, and a strict `COMPLIANCE & HYGIENE` section that enforces adherence to my coding standards by enumerating the working build environment, system and error handling, any dependencies, a directive to maintain strict ISO C99/C11 conformance and Unix LF-style line endings, not to invoke any shell commands and to use only the agents' native IDE tools (`view_file`, `grep_search`, `replace_file_content`, and `multi_replace_files_content`), a directive to never delete or remove any files, and depending on the subsystem being worked on either a directive to maintain hardware parity with the Xbox (e.g. "AC'97 Bus Master DMA must adhere to physical Intel ICH / MCPX AC'97 controller semantics" by referring to documented ground truths I provided) or arithmetic parity with the DSP (i.e. read the fucking manual). 

This is followed by a `SCOPE` section that lists which files, folders, headers, or sections can be explicitly touched either for audit or implementation (and if they're not in this scope section then they cannot be addressed). Lastly, there's a `CODE CONTEXT` header that delineates either my overall architectural direction, the phasing sequence of a particular objective if it is divided between multiple prompts, or reasons why we need to take a hard pivot by throwing away prior work or establishing new ground truths. Otherwise if I am ever architecting something new or rearchitecting something already done I always start with a separate reconnaissance phase that mandates strict, read-only static analysis before ever writing a single line of C so that I always have some form of documentation I can maintain to avoid context pollution, speculative drift, and ensuring all hypotheses are rigorously testable against hardware telemetry. ***Only after all of that*** does this templated prompt list the actual code tasking objectives for Antigravity to implement and the deliverables and verification I require, which always includes a code diff and an execution walkthrough artifact that details what it did and why. It should also be said that Antigravity itself is sandboxed into a dev drive, restricted to only the `xemu` folder for its workspace, with enforced file-access denies on `.git/***`, `build/***`, `subprojects/**`, and specific `tools/`, no internet usage, and request-only terminal access (normally denied). All models are prompted with `High` "thinking levels."

Compartmentalizing and containing everything this way is how you avoid an AI-assisted coding project collapsing into "vibecoding arson". Avoiding issuing an IDE agent an open-ended prompt by having web Gemini handle prompt synthesis allowed Antigravity to strictly act as a "junior dev" that only executes code instead of hallucinating across the entire repository. Along with the occasional RAG search to verify historical systems assumptions and baselines, this AI assistance allowed me to focus on the core systems engineering that the AI couldn't do. To that end I've found it faster to intentionally break perfectly good code by temporarily rearchitecting everything from scratch just to diagnose an edge-case issue with a game than by combing through Ghidra and endlessly breakpointing through gdb, though I certainly did plenty of that. At no point did I allow an issue that stymied progress to be blindly handled by AI instead of my own domain intuition and at no point did I succumb to "sunk cost fallacy" as the moment my own telemetry proved a better path existed I had no issues throwing away working code. This also extends to exporting and migrating LLM context to a fresh window the second I hit my stated objective long before I run out of buffer and encounter any hallucinatory drift. I described this as akin to setting a price target for selling a stock instead of gambling on an open-ended speculative run. No matter how good the flowstate might be that LLM must always be progress-gated so an accurate retrospective summary of its context can be generated for constant never-ending migration. In this way I have never once lost context coherency throughout the entire length of development and across multiple Gemini model upgrades.

In other projects that autonomously used AI to basically do the engineering itself I almost always see hallucinations (great or small) about working code because the AI can only read telemetry, it can't actually see and listen to the game like a live human playtester can. In the end, I found that synthetic tests only prove that code matches the test author's assumptions whereas actually manually playing weird retail games proves that code matches the console. Doubly so if those tests were written by a self-serving AI. One hacked together wrestling game (*WWE Raw 2*) is worth a thousand synthetic unit tests. *The Lord of the Rings: The Third Age* was my golden goose whose bizarre audio engine converted red herrings into clarified top-down architectural synthesis.

Where the LLMs allowed this project to finish in "only" three months (of daily work and multiple allnighters) rather than the 6 months to a year it actually would have taken me was building extremely precise telemetry hooks and trace harnesses when those edge case games absolutely needed to be nailed down. The ultimate victory here came from siccing Gemini 4 Argon via Antigravity on the proprietary Encode Processor `dolby_ep.bin` microcode (whose initial discovery, extraction, and subsequent reverse engineering was entirely my own human effort) in order to open source it for free redistribution with this fork. This was the proprietary firmware uploaded at runtime by `dsound.lib` (which I recovered from decompiling *Halo 2* in Ghidra), distinct from the separate 2,048-word Y-Data ROM (`Y:$0800–$0FFF`) physically fused into the DSP56362 silicon at the factory. All I had the AI do was yet another read-only static audit report like I've done before on the Xemu codebase and nothing more. However, in order to do that it basically coded, built, and ran a deterministic (based on all of my own docs I gave it) disassembler/tracer and then spent 1h 39m (my entire week's quota of tokens in one prompt lol) running 5 full simulation passes on the binary and then writing a [full report on its findings](/dsp56300-corpus/08_dolby_ep_prom_forensics.md) that revealed that `dolby_ep.bin` was *not* a flat program like I originally assumed but rather a dynamic, overlay-driven micro-operating system consisting of two resident kernel segments, a runtime scratch RAM heap allocator, and six relocatable overlays that get dynamically DMA-loaded from scratch RAM at runtime. It was my first time using this particular model so the prompt was also a test of its abilities and it impressively became probably the closest I've ever inadvertently gotten to "autonomous AI use."

Why it took so long to complete was that not only did the LLM successfully disassemble and analyze all the code, in the process of doing so it discovered about 6 real bugs within Xemu Symphony's C DSP interpreter that prevented some of those overlays from executing. Because it was restricted to read-only access on the emulator source (`hw/`) and therefore couldn't touch the codebase directly to fix the bugs in said interpreter it decided to spend an hour writing DSP56300 opcode implementations and disassembler guards ***inside the trace harness itself*** that it then integrated those bug fixes into in order to execute and profile every single instruction of all 6 overlays in `dolby_ep.bin`. Then it became *very* easy to simply follow the produced document to properly open source the Encode Processor (EP) P-ROM since I had an exact map of all `7,264` words of `dolby_ep.bin` and it turned out that fully 97.97% of all EP compute (`4,659,636` out of `4,756,404` cycles) was wasted on shit I didn't need nor want: Overlay #1 performed Pro Logic II downmixing, Overlay #2 performed 512-point MDCT, Overlay #3 did psychoacoustic bit allocation, and Overlay #4 did the AC-3 bitstream/CRC-16 packing.

All the guest Xbox driver actually depends on from the Encode Processor is its executive engine: maintaining the `P:$0001–$0007` mailbox (modulo-6 block counter, `0xCCCCCC` watchdog signature, frame heartbeat, and cycle delta), servicing the Segment 2 DMA and heap command dispatcher (including Bit-14 zero-padding on Output FIFO 0 and returning `success` on overlay-load requests), and yielding cleanly on `WAIT` (`0x000086`). Once that engine was documented down to the register level, authoring a clean-room 584-word DSP56362 micro-kernel (`symphony_ep_rom.h`) to replace the proprietary firmware - alongside a clean-room `ep_yrom.h` regenerated from public-domain ATSC A/52A math - was pretty straightforward. It satisfies every guest timing and DMA handshake in 35–50 cycles per subframe instead of the original 12,800 with zero proprietary code required. The full digital archaeology story of hunting down that retail binary across the Xbox library in the first place, along with the rest of the reverse engineering tale, I'll save for a separate development journal that I'm currently writing.

Where I think I diverged from upstream Xemu is that I realized the audio work is done in software and not by constantly dicking around with the hardware itself. The physical silicon on the Xbox motherboard was essentially two highly commodified 24-bit Motorola/Parthus DSPs hooked up to a 256-voice mixer and a scatter-gather DMA controller. What made the original Xbox's audio pipeline a 6th-gen powerhouse was the **software contract (`dsound.lib`, the Sensaura & I3DL2 GP microcode, and the Dolby Interactive EP executive)** running on top of those parts. The work was easy; the DSP microcode and firmware were written by professional DSP engineers and assembled with Motorola's `asm56300.exe` (which rejects illegal encodings at assemble time). Valid steady-state audio loops do not plant nested `ILLEGAL` opcodes inside stack-error handlers and do not execute unallocated opcodes, rather, software behaviors for audio systems instead typically handle issues through timer recoveries, launch options, or by monitoring audio session initialization instead of manifesting an infinite execution loop. The main thing AI enabled for me was to plug in my reverse engineering research into an existing emulator so that I can enjoy the fruits of my labor myself rather than just publishing my findings and hoping someone else implements it as was the original plan.

Frankly I think the only reason it took this long for anyone to work on the Xbox APU/MCPX was that the Venn diagram of "People who emulate the OG Xbox," "People who are audio engineers," and "People who understand legacy DirectSound3D-based hardware-accelerated audio for PC gaming" is basically just a dot. I already personally know of three examples where frontier AI models with maxed out subscriptions were given essentially infinite tokens, time, and local compute to do the same thing I did and it didn't even come close. Ultimately, I am not doing this for meaningless internet clout, I'm just doing it for video game preservation as it saddens me that so little attention has been paid towards the historically significant original Xbox console after two and a half decades. There are far more hidden gems trapped on this console to just be remembered for *Halo*. My hope is that proper emulation will hopefully let people discover what they missed out on and get the needle moving on improving low level emulation of the other subsystems. Until then I at least did my part to get the audio covered on my end.

AI is a tool, nothing more, but unlike your average hammer this tool gets legitimately flustered when there's no easy solution and will *absolutely* gaslight the situation in order to force a solution, otherwise it will simply rearrange the deck chairs and test ad nauseam until *something* works. In my experience it's less that the AI hallucinates itself and more that it attempts to deliberately hallucinate ***you*** if you're unable to tell when the model is rationalizing a dead end because you yourself don't know any better. Ultimately this project was only successful because I tested retail games, disassembled the ones that were breaking, compared with live retail hardware to verify how it's supposed to work (even sometimes comparing it to XeFu as well), and ensured that every architectural change is deterministically verified against the actual DSP silicon documentation.
